**1. The "Mile High" step keeps moving**
- Hook: "Denver has moved its mile-high mark three times, and it's moving again."
- Fact: On the Capitol's west steps, the 15th step is engraved "ONE MILE ABOVE SEA LEVEL". The original bronze markers from 1909 kept getting stolen, so the words were cut into the stone in 1947. In 1969, CSU engineering students put a brass benchmark on the 18th step. In 2003 a new survey found that one was 3.03 ft too high, and a brass plaque showing the Rocky Mountains went on the 13th step. As of Aug 1, 2026, CPR reported that a temporary marker was placed after a new National Geodetic Survey measurement, and that "a permanent one will be placed on the steps next year."
- Sources: https://5280.com/only-in-colorado-the-capitols-mile-high-steps/ · https://www.cpr.org/2026/06/26/denver-mile-high-marker-moving/ · https://www.cpr.org/2026/08/01/colorado-turns-150-with-cake-and-celebrations-at-the-state-capitol/ · https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/news/colorado-elevation-study-capitol-mile-high-marker-fourteeners-elevations/
- Place: Colorado State Capitol, west steps. The steps are white granite, and the dome is covered in 24-karat gold leaf.
- Risks: Don't name the new step (see UNCONFIRMED). Don't call any plaque "the official one". Say "as of September 2026".

**2. Denver was named to flatter a governor who had already quit**
- Hook: "Denver is named after a man who had already quit his job, and he hardly ever came here."
- Fact: The Denver City Town Company was founded Nov 22, 1858. Its founders named the town after Kansas Territorial Governor James W. Denver, hoping he would make it the county seat. About a month earlier, he had resigned to become Commissioner of Indian Affairs. He visited the city only twice, in 1875 and 1883 (corrected; see above). William Larimer named the main street after himself and the parallel streets after his associates in the company. The party included Curtis, Wynkoop and Blake.
- Sources: https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/who-was-city-denver-named-after-meet-general-james-w-denver · https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/larimer-square
- Place: Larimer St at 15th St, the site of Larimer's first cabin, now Larimer Square. Show street signs for Larimer, Curtis, Wynkoop and Blake.
- Risks: Don't say "Denver hated Denver". The library says he was mainly unhappy with how he was welcomed.

**3. Venice copied Denver's tower (the joke)**
- Hook: "Downtown Denver has a copy of a Venice bell tower, and it was built taller than the one in Venice."
- Fact: The Daniels & Fisher Tower opened in 1911, modeled on St. Mark's Campanile. The Campanile had collapsed in 1902 and was being rebuilt. Denver's tower was a few feet taller, and the Denver Times joked that Venice was building a smaller copy of Denver's tower. It is 330 ft tall, 375 ft counting the flagpole. It was Denver's tallest structure for more than 40 years. The department store beside it was demolished in 1970–71, but the tower was spared.
- Source: https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/daniels-and-fisher-tower
- Place: 16th St at Arapahoe. The tower is a slender campanile of brick and terra cotta over a steel frame, with four clock faces 16 ft across. CHECK its color in current photos.
- Risks: Say "one of the tallest in the country", not "the tallest".

**4. Denver owns a bison herd you can see from I-70**
- Hook: "Driving I-70? Those bison belong to the City of Denver."
- Fact: Denver keeps two conservation bison herds, at Genesee Park and Daniels Park. They descend from "the last wild bison in North America". By 1908, 18 bison at the Denver Zoo in City Park were all that remained in Colorado. The herd moved to Genesee in 1914 and to Daniels Park in 1938. Since April 2021 Denver has donated surplus bison to tribal nations.
- Sources: https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Parks-Recreation/Urban-Parks-Trails/Mountain-Parks/Bison-Conservation · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genesee_Park_(Colorado) (viewing spots only)
- Place: Genesee Park. There are overlooks on I-70 just west of exit 254, with pastures on both sides of the highway and ponderosa and Douglas-fir forest.
- Risks: Don't say "from Yellowstone" (see UNCONFIRMED). Don't give a head count.

**5. Red Rocks is older than the Rockies, and Denver owns it**
- Hook: "The rocks at Red Rocks come from mountains that disappeared before today's Rockies existed."
- Fact: The Fountain Formation is about 300 million years old. It is sediment washed off the Ancestral Rockies, which eroded away before today's Rockies rose, roughly 65 million years ago. A bronze plaque marks where this rock meets 1.7-billion-year-old basement rock. Ship Rock (south) and Creation Rock (north) are "taller than Niagara Falls". The amphitheatre is owned and operated by the City and County of Denver. It was built by the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC) to a design by Burnham Hoyt and opened June 15, 1941.
- Sources: https://www.redrocksonline.com/our-story/geology/ · https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/red-rocks-park-and-amphitheatre
- Place: Red Rocks Amphitheatre, Morrison. Tilted red-rust sandstone monoliths frame the stage and the seating bowl.
- Risks: The official site places Ship Rock on the south side, but the Colorado Encyclopedia page gives the order the other way, so the sides are uncertain. Avoid "ocean floor" geology. The Colorado Encyclopedia page also contains injected casino spam, so don't link it publicly.

**6. The 16th Street pavement is a rattlesnake**
- Hook: "Look down on 16th Street: the pattern under your feet is a rattlesnake."
- Fact: The street was designed by I.M. Pei & Partners (lead designer Henry Cobb, landscape by Laurie Olin) and opened Oct 4, 1982. The granite pattern was inspired by Navajo rugs and diamondback rattlesnake skin. The stones were charcoal gray from Minnesota, light gray from Massachusetts, and Colorado red. A $176M rebuild was finished in Oct 2025 with more than 950,000 new pavers, and the design "honors" the original pattern. In May 2025 the name "Mall" was dropped; it is now "16th Street".
- Sources: https://historicdenver.org/blog/16th-street-mall-public-art-of-the-highest-calibre/ · https://www.denver7.com/news/local-news/after-3-years-of-construction-denvers-16th-street-is-officially-reopened
- Place: 16th Street. Use only photos from after October 2025. Historic Denver says the new pavers are smaller and laid differently in the transit lanes and walking lanes.
- Risks: Don't say "the original stones". Don't promise the free shuttle (see UNCONFIRMED).

**7. Why downtown's streets are crooked**
- Hook: "Downtown Denver's streets are tilted because gold miners needed to reach the water."
- Fact: Downtown was laid out in line with Cherry Creek and the South Platte "to give claimants in the mining camp access to water". In 1878 the grid was switched to follow the compass. The awkward intersections where the two grids meet are what's left of the boomtown.
- Sources: https://www.museumofdenver.org/blog/why-are-downtown-denver-streets-diagonal · https://www.cpr.org/2018/12/03/arapahoe-to-zuni-your-questions-about-denver-streets-answered/
- Place: An overhead view of the seam where the diagonal downtown grid meets the north–south grid along Broadway and Colfax.
- Risks: Don't give a degree figure (see UNCONFIRMED). The two sources credit the change differently: the Museum says 1878, while CPR credits Henry C. Brown's Capitol Hill plat of 1868.

**8. Coors Field's purple row and the humidor**
- Hook: "One row of seats at Coors Field is purple, and the baseballs are kept in their own cooler."
- Fact: A band of purple seats in the 20th row of the upper deck marks one mile above sea level; the other seats are green. The air pressure is about 15% lower than at sea-level ballparks. Since 2002, the balls have been stored in a humidor, a 9×9×7-ft room kept at 70°F. The idea came from engineer Tony Cowell, who noticed his hunting boots dried out and shrank. MLB standardized how all 30 clubs store baseballs in 2018.
- Sources: https://sabr.org/bioproj/park/coors-field-denver/ · https://www.mlb.com/news/rockies-humidor-stories
- Place: Coors Field. The field sits below street level and has a clock tower, green seats, and one purple row high in the upper deck. CHECK the exterior materials.
- Risks: Don't give a humidity number (MLB says 50%, SABR says 40%). Don't claim the humidor "fixed" scoring.

**9. Union Station's secrets**
- Hook: "Denver once boarded trains underground, and a welcome arch lit by 2,200 bulbs stood outside."
- Fact: History Colorado says the 1881 station was the largest structure in the West. A fire in 1894 led to the 1914 rebuild. An underground boarding area was built in 1912, and some unused passages remain. In World War II the station handled 24,000 people a day. The Mizpah Arch, lit by 2,200 bulbs, stood over 17th St from 1906 until 1933; two benches still mark where it stood. The "Travel by Train" sign dates from the 1950s.
- Sources: https://www.historycolorado.org/story/classroom-resources/2019/04/05/6-things-you-didnt-know-about-union-station · https://www.denverunionstation.com/about/our-history/
- Place: Wynkoop at 17th St. The station is carved granite in the Beaux-Arts style, with rooftop "Union Station" and "Travel by Train" signs. CHECK the sign color; Wikipedia says orange.
- Risks: Don't give a tower height; the sources say 128 ft and 180 ft. The station's own site wrongly says the 1881 depot "stood less than three years". Say "1950s" for the sign.

**10. The newspaper built in a creek bed**
- Hook: "Denver's first newspaper office was built on stilts in a dry creek bed. Guess what happened."
- Fact: On the night of May 19, 1864, Cherry Creek flooded. The Rocky Mountain News building, "on stilts along the creek bed near Market Street", was lost, along with the only copies of some early issues. Old City Hall on Blake St was destroyed along with the city's earliest land records. At least 8 people were known to have died.
- Source: https://history.denverlibrary.org/news/denver/may-1864-brought-denvers-first-big-flood%E2%80%94and-swept-away-much-more
- Place: Cherry Creek where Larimer and Market Streets cross it, beside Larimer Square. Speer Blvd runs along the creek. I did not read what the channel looks like today.
- Risks: Don't say 19 dead. Don't claim "the Arapaho warned them" (see UNCONFIRMED).

**11. The Denver Mint is a copy of a Florentine palace**
- Hook: "The building making America's coins on Colfax is a copy of a Renaissance palace in Florence."
- Fact: The Mint building is modeled on Florence's Palazzo Medici Riccardi. Congress created it in 1862, but for more than 40 years it only tested gold and silver (an "assay office"). It struck its first coins in 1906; they were delayed because its machinery was on display at the 1904 St. Louis Exposition. In 1934 one-third of the nation's gold bullion was moved here from San Francisco. Denver and Philadelphia are the only mints that make coins for general circulation.
- Source: https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/denver-mint
- Place: W Colfax Ave between Cherokee and Delaware. The front is rough-cut Colorado granite with a red-tile roof, and the modern visitor entrance is on Cherokee.
- Risks: Don't lead with the "D" mint mark (see UNCONFIRMED). The Colorado Encyclopedia says the first year produced about 2.1 million coins; ignore the 167M figure you may see elsewhere.

**12. The "300 days of sunshine" myth**
- Hook: "Denver's famous 300 sunny days only add up if one hour of sun counts as a sunny day."
- Fact: The state climatologist said there is "no official definition". A 1990s study found about 300 days if any day with at least 1 hour of sun counts. By National Weather Service (NWS) definitions, Denver typically has about 115 clear, 130 partly cloudy and 120 cloudy days. The 2020 NWS summary counted 115 fair days. A 46-year federal climate data review found sun on 69% of days, about 252.
- Sources: https://www.cbsnews.com/colorado/news/300-days-of-sun-not-really-just-good-marketing/ (2015) · https://coloradosun.com/2024/05/06/300-days-of-sunshine-colorado/
- Place: There is no natural landmark for this; it needs a generic named viewpoint such as Civic Center Park. It is the weakest on the "specific place" rule.
- Risks: Don't claim who invented the slogan (see UNCONFIRMED).

**Alternates:**
- **Larimer Square:** The Kettle Building (1873), at 1426 Larimer, is the block's oldest surviving building. By 1900 the street had a skid-row reputation. In 1926 business owners tried to rename it "Main Street". Dana Crawford saved the block, and in 1971 it became Denver's first historic district. Source: https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/larimer-square
- **The 1972 Olympics vote:** Colorado voters rejected funding for the 1976 Winter Olympics on Nov 7, 1972. The Colorado Encyclopedia calls it "the first and only case" of a city refusing the Games after winning the IOC bid. It has no single place to film. Source: https://coloradoencyclopedia.org/article/1976-winter-olympics
- **Winter Park:** Denver created the ski area, which opened in January 1940 and was renamed from West Portal. The Forest Service permit is still held by the City and County, with a nonprofit association as its agent. Ownership is legally tangled, so don't say "Denver owns it". Sources: https://www.westword.com/news/denver-winter-park-ski-mountain-alterra-15231159/ (Dec 2023) · https://stories.grandcountyhistory.org/article/winter-park
- **Boiling point at altitude:** Water boils at 203°F at 5,000 ft versus 212°F at sea level, and a "3-minute" egg takes about 5 minutes. It has no natural landmark. Source: https://extension.colostate.edu/resource/high-elevation-food-preparation-guide/
